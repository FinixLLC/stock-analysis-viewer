## Business Profile

Source: live SEC EDGAR fetch — FY2026 10-K (fiscal year ended 2026-01-31, filed 2026-03-31), read and synthesized directly from Item 1 (Business) and Item 1A (Risk Factors) source text. No locally cached 10-K text dump existed for TJX in `company_profile/financial_reports/`.

What the company does: TJX is the leading off-price apparel and home fashions retailer in the U.S. and worldwide, operating over 5,200 stores plus six branded e-commerce sites, selling brand-name/designer merchandise generally 20–60% below full-price retailers' regular prices. Core differentiator is a "treasure hunt" flexible, opportunistic buying model (vs. traditional retailers' far-in-advance ordering) sourcing from ~21,000 vendors across 100+ countries.

Segments (as of FY2026, ~5,262 stores overall by Q1 FY2027):
- Marmaxx (U.S.) — TJ Maxx (founded 1976) and Marshalls (acquired 1995), 2,603 stores, the largest off-price retailer in the U.S.; also includes Sierra (145 stores, outdoor/active gear off-price).
- HomeGoods (U.S.) — HomeGoods (963 stores) and Homesense (79 stores), off-price home fashions.
- TJX Canada — Winners (316 stores), HomeSense (162 stores), Marshalls (111 stores).
- TJX International — TK Maxx (673 stores across UK, Ireland, Germany, Poland, Austria, Netherlands, and starting March 2026, Spain) and Homesense Europe (74 stores); also TK Maxx Australia (88 stores).

Moat, as described by the company:
- Opportunistic buying + lean inventory model — merchants stay in the market year-round (unlike traditional retailers that order far in advance), buying closeouts, special production, order cancellations, and manufacturer overruns; ~21,000 vendors provides scale and diversification of supply that's hard for smaller off-price competitors to replicate.
- Flexible, low-cost operating structure — low advertising spend (brand/banner-focused, not per-item promotion), no promotional discounting/coupons (pricing gap maintained structurally rather than through sales events), and a distribution/logistics network built for rapid inventory turns.
- Synergies across banners — shared buying expertise, vendor relationships, and best practices leveraged across all four segments and the Sierra business.
- Vendor-friendly reputation — company states it's an attractive channel for vendors because it purchases less-than-full assortments, pays promptly, doesn't demand advertising/markdown/delivery concessions, and has an excellent credit rating — a structural advantage in sourcing that reinforces the buying-scale moat.

Risks the company itself flags as most material (Item 1A, "Operational and Strategic Risks" listed first):
1. Execution risk in the opportunistic buying/inventory strategy itself — the top-listed risk. The model depends on merchants (with considerable individual discretion) correctly assessing merchandise value, timing, quantities, and store allocation; failure to maintain the pricing "value gap" versus conventional retailers, or inaccurate sales forecasting, directly threatens sales/margins — i.e., the core competitive strength is also explicitly named as the top vulnerability if execution falters.
2. Intense competition — competes with local, regional, national, and international department, specialty, off-price, discount, warehouse, and outlet retailers across brand, fashion, price, quality, selection, freshness, and shopping experience.
3. Large global workforce dependency (~377,000 Associates as of Jan 31, 2026, ~86% in stores, many under collective bargaining agreements in the U.S./Canada distribution network and works councils in Europe) — implicit labor-relations and staffing-execution risk given the model's reliance on rapid inventory processing/turnover.
