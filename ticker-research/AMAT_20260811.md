## Business Profile

Source: FY2025 10-K, fetched live via SEC EDGAR this session (`https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/6951/000162828025056742/amat-20251026.htm`) — no local cache existed for AMAT; read and synthesized directly from Item 1 (Business) and Item 1A (Risk Factors) source text.

What the company does: Applied Materials describes itself as "the leader in the materials engineering solutions used to produce virtually every semiconductor in the world." It designs, develops, manufactures, sells and services the wafer-fabrication equipment used to make semiconductor chips. Incorporated 1967, Delaware corporation, fiscal year ends the last Sunday in October.

Segments (FY2025):
- Semiconductor Systems (73% of revenue, $20.798B): equipment across patterning, transistor/interconnect fabrication, process control (optical/eBeam inspection and metrology), and advanced packaging (heterogeneous integration/chiplet connections). Sold into foundry/logic (67% of segment revenue), DRAM (26%) and NAND flash (7%) end markets.
- Applied Global Services (AGS) (23%, $6.385B): services, spares, and factory-automation software for the installed base of fabs globally — a recurring-revenue-like business tied to the size of AMAT's cumulative equipment installed base rather than new capex cycles, providing some counter-cyclical ballast. (Note: the 200mm equipment business is being moved from AGS into Semiconductor Systems starting fiscal 2026.)
- Corporate and Other (4%, $1.185B): includes the display-equipment business.

Moat, as described in the company's own words:
- Breadth and integration: "the semiconductor capital equipment industry's most comprehensive portfolio of products," with the explicit ability to "combine, co-optimize and integrate" technologies across process steps — a differentiation strategy versus single-product-line competitors.
- IP scale: more than 23,500 active patents, though the company states it is not materially dependent on any single one.
- Deep, geographically diverse customer collaboration model, with product development concentrated in the U.S., India and Israel and customer demonstration centers in the U.S., China, Taiwan, Israel and South Korea.
- AGS installed-base servicing model provides a recurring revenue stream layered on top of the more cyclical equipment sales business.

Management's stated priorities: developing products that solve customer technology-inflection challenges (explicitly citing AI as a "significant demand driver," alongside IoT, robotics, EVs/AVs and clean energy), growing the services (AGS) business, and expanding served-market opportunities within semiconductors — continued heavy RD&E investment to stay ahead of demand rather than react to it.

Risks the company itself flags as most material (Item 1A, in the company's own ordering):
1. Industry cyclicality and demand forecasting difficulty — explicitly ties this to AI demand specifically: "AI is evolving rapidly and the expected timing and amount of investments related to AI can change significantly. As a result, it is difficult to accurately forecast demand for our products related to AI."
2. Macro/economic uncertainty — tariffs, trade policy, interest rates, inflation, bank failures cited as factors that can cause customers to delay/cancel equipment purchases.
3. Geopolitical/trade risk, named explicitly and in detail: "a majority of our products and services are delivered to customers in jurisdictions outside of the United States, including China, Taiwan and Korea," with U.S. and foreign trade restrictions, export controls and tariffs cited as live, ongoing risks — not hypothetical, given the FY2025 10-K's own China revenue decline.
4. Active government inquiries: the company discloses that "since 2022, we have received multiple subpoenas from government authorities requesting information relating to certain China customer shipments and export controls compliance," including from the DOJ, Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security, and the SEC — an ongoing, unresolved legal/regulatory exposure (consistent with the separately-reported $253M BIS settlement noted in current news below).
5. Competition from China-based domestic equipment manufacturers, which the company states could be aided by "local government incentives and funding" as well as by the very U.S. export controls meant to restrict AMAT's own China sales (a somewhat paradoxical dynamic the company explicitly acknowledges: U.S. export controls "may also provide an advantage to our international competitors").
6. Customer concentration: two customers represented ~19% and ~15% of FY2025 revenue — a real dependency on a handful of the world's largest fabs.
7. Single/limited-source supplier risk: "some key parts may be obtained from only a qualified single supplier or a limited group of qualified suppliers."
