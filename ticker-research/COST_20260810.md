## Business Profile

Source: locally-fetched FY2025 10-K (fiscal year ended 2025-08-31, filed 2025-10-08) via live SEC EDGAR pull — read and synthesized directly from Item 1 (Business) and Item 1A (Risk Factors) source text. (No local cache of this filing existed in `company_profile/financial_reports/`; fetched fresh this session per the standard fallback procedure.)

What the company does: Costco operates membership warehouse clubs (914 locations at FY2025 year-end, since grown to 933 per the July 2026 sales release) across the U.S., Canada, Mexico, Japan, the U.K., Korea, Australia, Taiwan, China, Spain, France, Sweden, Iceland, and New Zealand, plus e-commerce sites in most of those markets. The core business model: offer a deliberately limited selection (fewer than 4,000 active SKUs per warehouse in the core business, vs. far more at typical broadline retailers; 9,000-10,000 SKUs online) of nationally-branded and private-label products at low prices, driving high sales volume and rapid inventory turnover. This lets Costco operate profitably at significantly lower gross margins than most retailers — a deliberate low-margin, high-velocity model rather than a margin-maximizing one. Average warehouse size is ~147,000 sq ft. Merchandise is bought largely direct from suppliers and routed through cross-docking depots rather than traditional multi-step distribution, reducing handling costs.

Revenue/format mix: two revenue streams — net sales (merchandise across Foods & Sundries, Non-Foods, and Fresh Foods, plus warehouse ancillary businesses like gasoline/pharmacy/optical/food court/hearing aids/tire installation, and other businesses like e-commerce/business centers/travel) and membership fees, which are a smaller but high-margin, high-visibility recurring revenue stream ($1.373B in the most recent quarter alone, +10.8% YoY) that underpins the entire membership-club economics.

Moat, as described in the company's own words and evidenced by the numbers: (1) scale-driven purchasing power and operating efficiency (low SKU count, high inventory turnover, no-frills warehouses, shorter operating hours) that let Costco sustainably underprice most competitors; (2) the membership format itself is explicitly described as "integral to our business and profitability," reinforcing loyalty and providing a continuing fee-revenue stream — renewal rates of 92.2% (U.S./Canada) and 89.7% (worldwide) in the most recent quarter are direct evidence this moat is intact; (3) Kirkland Signature, the private-label brand, is explicitly cited as high-quality, generally lower-priced than national brands, and typically higher-margin — a genuine differentiated-brand asset; (4) strict entrance/exit control combined with the membership format is cited as keeping inventory shrinkage well below typical retail levels.

Management's operational discipline (from Item 1): Costco explicitly frames its low-price strategy as durable because of structural cost advantages (lower labor costs relative to sales volume, efficient warehouse layout, reduced handling), not promotional discounting — a distinction the company draws deliberately.

Named competitive landscape (company's own disclosure): Walmart, Target, Kroger, and Amazon are cited as significant general-merchandise retail competitors in the U.S.; Walmart's Sam's Club and BJ's Wholesale Club are named as the direct warehouse-club-format competitors — many U.S. metro areas and some international markets have multiple competing clubs.

Risks the company itself flags as most material (Item 1A, company's own framing):
1. Geographic concentration risk — the company is explicitly "highly dependent" on U.S. and Canadian operations (86% and 84% of net sales/operating income in FY2025 respectively), and within the U.S., specifically dependent on California (26% of U.S. net sales) — a striking single-state concentration the company calls out directly as a risk factor.
2. Growth-execution risk — expansion depends on Costco's ability to acquire property, secure permits, and build/lease new warehouses/depots against local land-use restrictions, community opposition, and (notably) increasing difficulty securing long-term utility contracts for new buildings amid rising electricity/water demand and grid stress in certain regions — a fairly specific, currently-relevant infrastructure constraint disclosed by the company.
3. Cannibalization risk — the company explicitly acknowledges that new warehouses in existing markets can draw members away from existing locations, pressuring comparable sales at the cannibalized stores even as overall market share grows.
4. Regulatory risk to the warehouse-club format itself — the 10-K notes that certain jurisdictions have enacted or proposed laws restricting the operation or expansion of large retailers and warehouse clubs specifically, a targeted regulatory risk beyond generic retail regulation.
