## Business Profile

Source: live WebSearch of TSMC's own quarterly investor disclosures and financial media coverage (no locally cached 20-F/10-K was available this session).

What the company does: TSMC is the world's largest dedicated semiconductor foundry, manufacturing chips designed by other companies (the "fabless" model) rather than designing and selling its own branded chips. It offers leading-edge logic process technologies — 2-nanometer ("N2," Nanosheet Transistor) and 3-nanometer (FinFET) among the most advanced in production — serving both fabless IC design companies and system companies directly.

Revenue mix / structural shift (Q2 2026): HPC (high-performance computing — AI accelerators, GPUs, data-center chips) now dominates at 66% of revenue, up sharply from 55% just two quarters earlier (Q4 2025) and 61% one quarter earlier (Q1 2026). Smartphone remains meaningful (22%) but is now clearly secondary. Automotive, though small (4%), is growing fast (+15% QoQ). Financial commentary (Hardware Busters, 2026-08) characterizes this as "not a cyclical swing — a structural recomposition," with AI training/inference demand from hyperscalers pulling TSMC's revenue center of gravity decisively toward high-performance silicon.

Moat: TSMC's position rests on process-technology leadership (2nm/3nm nodes in volume production ahead of competitors), massive scale (~70% global foundry market share in 2025, $122.5B revenue vs. Samsung's ~7.2% share/$12.6B), and deep, multi-year customer relationships with essentially every major fabless chip designer (NVIDIA, AMD, Apple, Broadcom, Marvell, Xilinx/AMD, and others — confirmed via the EVCDM graph below).

Geographic/manufacturing footprint expansion: TSMC is significantly expanding its U.S. presence — the Arizona program has scaled to an estimated ~$465B multi-decade investment across a planned 11 coordinated fab phases (front-end logic, specialty nodes for automotive/defense, and advanced packaging), following a U.S.-Taiwan chip-tariff framework finalized April 27, 2026. Notably, TSMC's Arizona fab has reportedly achieved higher yields than the Taiwan equivalent — a meaningful operational proof point for the overseas expansion strategy, though overseas fabs are also cited as a near-term gross-margin dilution factor (see Fundamentals section).

Competitive landscape: Samsung Electronics (~7.2% foundry share, but landed a $16.5B, 2025-2033 foundry contract with Tesla — the largest single-client long-term foundry deal to date, a genuine competitive threat signal) and Intel Foundry (smaller scale, but reportedly working with Musk's Terafab venture) are the two named alternative foundries; large customers like Apple are reported to be exploring dual-sourcing with Intel and Samsung, which — if it materializes at scale — would represent a real diversification risk to TSMC's near-monopoly position in leading-edge nodes.

Risks flagged by outside analysts and financial media (no local 20-F to cite the company's own Item 3D risk-factor language this session):
1. Geopolitical/Taiwan concentration risk — the dominant, recurring theme across coverage: TSMC is "expensive, capacity-constrained, and geopolitically risky," and the AI industry broadly is characterized as "too important to bet everything on a single supplier concentrated in Taiwan." China is described as holding real economic leverage over Taiwan given how central TSMC's chips are to the global economy.
2. Export-control/compliance exposure — a recent report (NBC News) flagged that TSMC-made chip components were found in a powerful AI chip built for a Chinese company, raising potential U.S. export-control violation concerns under scrutiny by semiconductor research firms — an active, unresolved compliance risk.
3. Tariff exposure on the Arizona buildout — supplies shipped from Taiwanese companies into the Arizona site reportedly face a 20% U.S. tariff on Taiwanese exports, a notably higher rate than the 15% applied to Japanese/South Korean exports, which could make some Taiwanese sub-suppliers less competitive in the broader global market even as TSMC itself localizes production.
4. Margin dilution from geographic/technology transition — the 2nm ramp and non-Taiwan fab mix are both cited as near-term gross-margin headwinds (potentially up to ~700bps peak impact per one analyst estimate), even though TSMC's own guidance still points to historically strong 65-67% gross margins.

## Bottom Line

TSMC delivered an exceptionally strong Q2 2026 (revenue +36% YoY, net income/EPS +77.4% YoY) driven by a rapid, structural shift toward AI/HPC demand — now 66% of revenue, up from 55% just two quarters prior — and backed that up with Q3 guidance (+37% YoY at midpoint) and a raised full-year capex plan ($60-64B, up at least $4B), signaling management's own confidence that AI-chip demand is not a temporary cyclical spike. The company's competitive position, independently corroborated by the EVCDM supply-chain graph, is close to unrivaled: TSMC is the disclosed, named foundry dependency for NVIDIA, AMD, Broadcom, Marvell, and Xilinx, and even competitor Intel discloses relying on TSMC for parts of its own product line — a rare "even my rival needs me" moat signal. The counterweight is geopolitical and structural rather than operational: persistent Taiwan-concentration risk (repeatedly flagged across financial media as the single biggest overhang on the stock), an active and unresolved export-control compliance question involving a Chinese customer, and a genuine (if still nascent) diversification threat from Samsung's $16.5B Tesla foundry win and reports that Apple is exploring dual-sourcing with Intel/Samsung. TSMC's ~$465B Arizona expansion is a credible, already-yielding-well hedge against the Taiwan-concentration risk, but it comes with disclosed near-term gross-margin dilution (2nm ramp + overseas fab mix, potentially up to ~700bps peak impact per one estimate) and tariff friction for its Taiwanese supplier ecosystem. PaoBull's cache (generated 2026-07-29 at $392.31, composite score 63.8/100 — the lowest of this batch, driven by a weak 45/100 market sub-score, no formal buy/hold/sell verdict) explicitly called the stock "tactically unattractive" on valuation despite endorsing the long-term moat thesis; the stock has since risen to $421.35, closing roughly two-thirds of the gap to PaoBull's $473.52 target and pushing well above its modeled entry/add-on levels — meaning the valuation caution flagged in that thesis is, if anything, more relevant today than when the cache was generated, even as the underlying AI-demand fundamentals have continued to strengthen.
