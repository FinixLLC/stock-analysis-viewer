## Business Profile

Source: locally cached FY2025 10-K (`company_profile/financial_reports/GEV_10-K_20260708.txt`) — read and synthesized directly from Item 1 (Business) and Item 1A (Risk Factors) source text.

What the company does: GE Vernova is a global electric-power-industry company spun off from General Electric (completed April 2, 2024; GE itself continues as GE Aerospace). It designs, manufactures, delivers, and services technologies that generate, transfer, orchestrate, convert, and store electricity. The company states its installed base generates approximately 25% of the world's electricity — a striking scale claim that underpins its competitive position.

Three reportable segments:
1. Power — gas turbines (heavy-duty and aeroderivative), nuclear power (boiling water reactor technology, fuel/services, small modular reactor JV with Hitachi), hydro power, and steam power (largely nuclear and coal-fired plant service/transition support). As of FY2025: ~$94.4B in remaining performance obligations (RPO), ~7,000-unit gas turbine installed base (~1,800 under long-term service agreements, ~10-year average remaining contract life), 126 HA-turbines installed with ~3.6M operating hours (since grown to 130 units/4M+ hours per the Q2 2026 update above). Company explicitly frames gas power as "essential" to the energy transition as a dispatchable complement to intermittent renewables, and cites rising electricity demand from hyperscalers and data centers as a demand driver.
2. Wind — onshore and offshore wind turbines and blades (including the LM Wind Power blade business). Workhorse products account for ~75% of equipment RPO; ~24,000 onshore turbines under service agreements out of a ~59,000-unit installed base. U.S. market is ~60% of Onshore Wind's equipment RPO — a concentration the company flags as exposed to tariff and production-tax-credit policy volatility. Offshore Wind is under pressure from project cost/execution-timeline challenges, and the company explicitly discloses that the U.S. Department of the Interior paused leases for all large-scale offshore wind projects under construction as of December 22, 2025, directly impacting the Vineyard Wind project timeline — a concrete, named regulatory headwind.
3. Electrification — grid solutions (HVDC transmission, AC substations, power transformers, switchgear, synchronous condensers, grid automation), power conversion & storage, and electrification software. Company describes "robust demand," citing large-scale transmission equipment needs to interconnect renewables and electrify data centers supporting AI, plus grid modernization broadly. Customer lead times have lengthened as demand outstrips supply — a capacity-constraint dynamic, not a demand problem.

Moat, as described in the company's own words: breadth/scale of technology portfolio across the full electric-power value chain (generation, transmission, storage) is positioned as the key differentiator versus narrower competitors; a large, long-lived installed base (gas turbines with ~10-year average remaining service contracts) provides a recurring, high-visibility service revenue stream.

Management's stated strategic priorities (from Item 1): deliver reliable/affordable/sustainable electricity; deepen relationships with major utilities/developers/governments; service the installed base while improving underwriting discipline to reduce risk on new deals; streamline the product portfolio around fewer "workhorse" products; apply lean methodology to cost structure; invest in new decarbonization technology (e.g., a first commercial direct air capture deployment using proprietary solid-sorbent technology); and return at least 1/3 of cash generation to stockholders.

Risks the company itself flags as most material (Item 1A, company's own framing):
1. Product quality/safety risk inherent to complex industrial machinery (gas turbines, wind turbines, grid infrastructure, nuclear equipment) — failures could cause injury, widespread outages, or major warranty/damage claims.
2. Supply chain and logistics disruption — explicit acknowledgment of operating in a "supply-constrained environment," with certain inputs (semiconductor chips, specialty metals, rare earths) sole-sourced or concentrated in a single country.
3. Policy/regulatory volatility, concretely instanced by the Interior Department's offshore wind lease pause and by tariff/production-tax-credit uncertainty affecting the ~60%-U.S.-weighted Onshore Wind backlog — and, per the Q2 2026 update, an explicit $100–$200M 2026 tariff cost estimate.
4. Execution/project risk in Offshore Wind specifically, where the company discloses ongoing cost and timeline pressure on its backlog.

Competitive landscape named explicitly by the company: Power segment — Siemens Energy, Mitsubishi Power, Westinghouse, Framatome, Rolls-Royce. Wind segment — Vestas, Siemens-Gamesa, Nordex, Envision, Goldwind. Electrification segment — Hitachi Energy, Siemens Energy, Siemens, Schneider Electric, Mitsubishi Electric, ABB.
