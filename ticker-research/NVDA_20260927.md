## Business Profile
Source: 10-K (NVDA_10-K_20260906.txt)

NVIDIA has evolved from a pioneer in accelerated computing into a data center-scale AI infrastructure company that reshapes industries by providing the foundational hardware and software stack for computationally intensive workloads. The company’s business model is built on a platform strategy that unifies hardware, systems, software, algorithms, and services to address four primary markets: Data Center, Gaming, Professional Visualization, and Automotive. In the Data Center segment, which drove the majority of fiscal year 2026 revenue growth, NVIDIA sells extreme co-designed infrastructure—integrating GPUs, CPUs, networking, and software—that allows customers to interconnect hundreds of thousands of GPUs into single, giant computers for AI training and inference. This approach is supported by the CUDA development platform and a broad ecosystem of libraries and APIs, creating a unified underlying programmable architecture that serves diverse end markets. The company reports results in two segments: Compute Networking, which includes Data Center and Automotive solutions, and Graphics, which covers GeForce gaming GPUs and professional visualization products.

The company’s competitive moat is derived from its deep software stack and ecosystem lock-in, specifically the CUDA platform, which facilitates the deployment of accelerated computing for AI, data analytics, and scientific computing. NVIDIA emphasizes that its large and growing number of developers and installed base strengthens the value of its platform, creating a barrier to entry for competitors. Furthermore, the company leverages a "full-stack" innovation approach, investing in silicon, systems, networking, and software to deliver order-of-magnitude performance advantages over legacy CPU-only approaches. This is evidenced by its dominance in high-performance computing, where NVIDIA powers over 78% of the supercomputers on the global TOP500 list. The company also maintains a rapid product cadence, such as the transition from Hopper to Blackwell and the upcoming Rubin platform, to maintain technological leadership in AI infrastructure.

Management’s stated priorities center on advancing the NVIDIA accelerated computing platform to solve complex problems with lower power consumption and faster processing than alternative approaches. A key strategic focus is the execution of Data Center compute product introductions on a one-year product cadence, including the scaling of the Blackwell Ultra platform and the launch of the Rubin platform for agentic and reasoning AI. Additionally, management prioritizes expanding the AI ecosystem through significant capital deployment; in fiscal year 2026, the company invested $17.5 billion in private companies and infrastructure funds, primarily to support early-stage startups and AI model makers that purchase NVIDIA products. The company also aims to strengthen its supply chain resiliency by expanding manufacturing capabilities into the U.S. and Latin America, moving away from a concentration in Asia.

The filing flags several material risks, most notably the dependency of revenue growth on the availability of data centers, energy, and capital for customer buildouts. Management explicitly states that shortages in these resources could delay deployments and reduce the scale of AI adoption. A significant financial risk materialized in fiscal year 2026 with a $4.5 billion charge associated with excess inventory and purchase obligations for the H20 product, resulting from U.S. government export license requirements for the China market. The filing also highlights risks related to supply chain complexity, noting that product transitions and sophisticated system configurations have caused production delays, revenue volatility, and quality issues. Furthermore, the company identifies the rise of high-quality open-source foundation models as a competitive threat, as these models could reduce demand for NVIDIA’s products if deployed on competitors' platforms. Finally, macroeconomic factors, including tariffs, inflation, and geopolitical developments, are cited as having direct and indirect impacts on supply chain costs, demand, and competitive position.
