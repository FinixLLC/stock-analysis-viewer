What the company does: Sandisk is "a leading developer, manufacturer and provider of data storage devices and solutions based on NAND flash technology," offering solid-state drives (SSDs), embedded products, removable cards, USB drives, and wafers/components across three end markets: Cloud (datacenter/cloud/private-cloud AI workloads), Client (OEM/channel across PC, mobile, gaming, automotive, VR/industrial), and Consumer (retail/branded products leveraging the well-known "SanDisk" consumer brand).

Corporate history and structure: spun off from Western Digital in February 2025 (details above) — this is a critical context point for understanding the company's currently thin standalone financial track record and its still-evolving independent supplier/procurement structure.

Moat, as described in the company's own words: patent/IP portfolio (~7,900 granted patents, ~3,200 pending applications) plus non-patented proprietary process technology, described as "an important factor in our success." The company explicitly states it relies on non-disclosure agreements and internal safeguards to protect proprietary information, acknowledging competitors "may obtain and use such information" despite these protections — a candid acknowledgment that IP protection is imperfect. The more structurally important moat element, however, is the Flash Ventures manufacturing joint venture relationship (historically with Kioxia) that supplies "substantially all" of Sandisk's flash memory wafers — this is simultaneously a moat (scaled, established NAND fab capacity/technology access) and a concentration risk (the company doesn't fully control its own primary manufacturing input).

Management's stated approach: post-separation, the company describes "independently maintain[ing] strategic relationships with Flash Ventures and other key suppliers" under "the Company's standalone procurement structure," balancing "business continuity, cost efficiency, and risk mitigation" — language that reads as a company actively working to establish independent operational footing rather than one that has fully completed that transition.

Risks the company itself flags as most material (Item 1A "Summary of Risk Factors," company's own category structure):
1. Operational Risks: adverse global/regional conditions; supply-chain disruption or increased materials/component costs; substantial risk of operational damage/disruption; public health crises; talent attraction/retention; product-defect/warranty/litigation exposure; cybersecurity/data-breach risk; and — notably — explicit acknowledgment that "we may be adversely affected by risks and challenges associated with the use of artificial intelligence," a direct AI-specific risk category similar to Micron's.
2. Business and Strategic Risks: reliance on strategic relationships (i.e., Flash Ventures, directly corroborating the supply-concentration risk above); competitive pressure including "declining average selling prices, volatile demand, technological change, industry consolidation and lengthy supply qualifications"; technology-transition/product-development execution risk; sales seasonality/cyclicality with acknowledged forecasting difficulty; and loss-of-key-customer risk.
3. Financial Risks: debt levels potentially restricting operations/flexibility; currency-exchange-rate exposure; customer credit risk.
4. Legal and Compliance Risks: data-use/security regulatory compliance; various customer/industry/coalition-driven compliance standards.

This risk profile is broadly consistent with Micron's (both are NAND/DRAM memory companies facing the same average-selling-price-volatility dynamic), but Sandisk's Flash Ventures dependency and very recent spin-off status are distinguishing structural features not shared with Micron, which is a vertically-integrated, long-established, wholly-owned manufacturer.
