## Business Profile
Source: 10-K (NVDA_10-K_20260906.txt)

NVIDIA has transitioned from a graphics processor manufacturer to a data center-scale AI infrastructure company, operating through two primary segments: Compute Networking and Graphics. Its business model is built on a platform strategy that integrates hardware, systems, software, algorithms, and services to solve computationally intensive problems. The core of this stack is the CUDA development platform, which supports a broad ecosystem of libraries, SDKs, and AI models. This unified, programmable architecture allows NVIDIA to serve diverse multi-billion-dollar markets—including Data Center, Gaming, Professional Visualization, and Automotive—with shared underlying technology. In the Data Center segment, the company delivers extreme co-designed infrastructure, such as the Blackwell and upcoming Rubin platforms, which combine GPUs, CPUs, DPUs, and high-performance networking (NVLink, InfiniBand, Ethernet) to function as single, massive computers for AI training and inference. The Graphics segment continues to serve gaming and workstation markets with GeForce and RTX PRO GPUs, increasingly leveraging AI features like DLSS and neural graphics.

The company’s competitive moat is derived from its deep software ecosystem and the resulting network effects. NVIDIA cites the large and growing number of developers and installed base as key strengths that increase the value of its platform for customers. By maintaining a unified underlying architecture, NVIDIA can leverage its research and development investments across multiple end markets, creating order-of-magnitude performance advantages over legacy CPU-only approaches. The company emphasizes that its full-stack innovation—spanning silicon, systems, networking, software, and algorithms—allows it to outpace Moore’s Law and deliver solutions for problems previously deemed unsolvable. Additionally, NVIDIA holds a dominant position in high-performance computing, powering over 78% of the supercomputers on the global TOP500 list, which reinforces its leadership in scientific computing and AI workloads.

Management’s stated priorities center on advancing the accelerated computing platform to deliver continued performance leaps and lower power consumption. A key strategic focus is maintaining a one-year product cadence for Data Center compute introductions, as evidenced by the transition from Hopper to Blackwell and the upcoming Rubin platform. NVIDIA is also prioritizing the expansion of its AI ecosystem through significant financial investments, including $17.5 billion in private companies and infrastructure funds to support early-stage startups and AI model makers. Furthermore, the company is working to strengthen supply chain resilience by expanding manufacturing capabilities into the U.S. and Latin America, moving away from a concentration in Asia. Management also highlights the importance of supporting the build-out of complex data center infrastructures, including providing guarantees for land, power, and shell to partners, to facilitate the adoption of AI infrastructure.

The filing flags several material risks, primarily related to external dependencies and regulatory constraints. A significant risk is the availability of data centers, energy, and capital required for customers to build out AI infrastructure; shortages in these resources could delay deployments and reduce demand. The company also highlights the complexity of product transitions and sophisticated system configurations, which can lead to production delays, supply and demand management challenges, revenue volatility, and quality issues. Regulatory risks are prominent, particularly regarding export controls; for instance, U.S. government licensing requirements for the H20 product in China resulted in a $4.5 billion charge for excess inventory and purchase obligations. Additionally, the rise of high-quality open-source foundation models poses a competitive threat, as these models could reduce demand for NVIDIA’s products if deployed on competitors’ platforms. Finally, the company notes that its supply chain is heavily concentrated in Asia, and while it is diversifying, the ability to ramp production in new regions depends on local manufacturing ecosystems meeting volume and timing requirements.
