## Business Profile
Source: 10-K (ORCL_10-K_20260906.txt)

Oracle operates as a provider of enterprise information technology frameworks, delivering products and services that build, run, and support IT environments through a hybrid model of cloud-based, on-premise, and hybrid deployments. The company’s business model is anchored in three distinct segments: cloud and software, which accounted for 87% of total revenues in fiscal 2026; hardware, representing 5%; and services, representing 8%. The cloud and software segment generates revenue through subscription-based cloud applications and infrastructure, perpetual software licenses, and recurring software support contracts, with cloud revenues comprising 51% of total company revenue in fiscal 2026, up from 37% in fiscal 2024. Oracle emphasizes flexibility and interoperability, allowing customers to deploy technologies across Oracle Cloud, other public clouds, or on-premise environments, a strategy management views as a key differentiator against competitors. The company invests heavily in research and development, spending $10.3 billion in fiscal 2026, to integrate artificial intelligence, automation, and analytics into its portfolio, including Oracle Cloud Applications (OCA) and Oracle Cloud Infrastructure (OCI).

Oracle’s competitive moat is derived from its comprehensive, integrated suite of applications and infrastructure technologies, which management argues differentiates the company from competitors offering more limited or specialized solutions. The company leverages a deep installed base of on-premise and cloud-based applications, facilitating a migration of existing customers to the Oracle Cloud, a trend that contributed $5.4 billion to annualized cloud revenue growth over the past three fiscal years. Additionally, Oracle claims a position of strength in infrastructure technologies, citing Oracle AI Database and MySQL as the world’s most popular database management systems and Java as the most widely used language for cloud-native development. The company’s ability to offer a unified data model across core business functions, such as ERP, HCM, and CX, creates stickiness by removing business boundaries between front- and back-office activities and reducing the complexity and risk of customer IT infrastructures.

Management’s stated priorities center on expanding the business through the continued migration of the existing customer base from on-premise to cloud deployments, increasing the proportion of cloud revenues, and leveraging AI to enhance workflow automation and innovation. The company prioritizes maintaining a broad, comprehensive, and flexible deployment model to address customer needs relative to competitors, while also focusing on improving the performance, security, and cost-effectiveness of its offerings. Oracle emphasizes direct sales through its worldwide sales force while also utilizing indirect channels, and it maintains a selective acquisition program to integrate complementary technologies and products in furtherance of its corporate strategy. The company expects total cloud and software revenues to continue increasing on a constant currency basis, driven by growth in cloud offerings and continued demand for software, which supports ongoing investments in R&D and cloud capacity expansion.

The filing flags several material risks, primarily related to macroeconomic and geopolitical factors, including the effects of inflation, tariffs, trade policy, and general economic conditions on customer demand and costs. Oracle notes that its margins may be adversely impacted by increases in supply chain and energy costs, the inability to pass tariff-related costs to customers, and foreign currency rate fluctuations. Additionally, the company highlights risks associated with governmental budgetary constraints, particularly for government agencies and educational institutions, and the competitive position of its offerings relative to other cloud infrastructure providers. The filing also identifies the uncertainty of quarterly hardware revenues due to the timing of large transactions and the finite life of hardware products, as well as the critical accounting estimate uncertainty surrounding worldwide income tax provisions, where final tax outcomes may differ from estimates due to complex global transactions and preferential tax treatments.
