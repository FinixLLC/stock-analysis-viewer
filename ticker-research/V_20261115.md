## Business Profile
Source: 10-K (V_10-K_20260827.txt)

Visa operates as a global payments technology company that facilitates secure, reliable, and efficient commerce and money movement through a four-party model connecting consumers, issuing and acquiring financial institutions, and sellers. The company is explicitly not a financial institution; it does not issue cards, extend credit, or bear credit risk, but rather earns revenue by providing transaction processing services—specifically authorization, clearing, and settlement—via its proprietary VisaNet network. Visa’s business model relies on a "network of networks" strategy and a "Visa as a Service" stack, which modularizes core capabilities like authentication and risk management into reusable components accessible via APIs and other integration methods. In fiscal 2025, the company processed 258 billion transactions with a total payments and cash volume of $17 trillion, generating $40 billion in net revenue.

Visa’s competitive moat is anchored in its massive global scale and network effects, evidenced by nearly 5 billion payment credentials, over 175 million merchant locations, and connections to approximately 12 billion cards, bank accounts, and digital wallets across more than 200 countries. This extensive infrastructure creates high switching costs and deep integration into the global financial system. Additionally, the company leverages proprietary technology, including Visa Token Service (VTS) with over 16 billion provisioned tokens, and advanced risk management and fraud prevention capabilities that differentiate it in cross-border travel and digital commerce. The filing highlights Visa’s early adoption of artificial intelligence and its investment in next-generation technologies such as generative AI, stablecoins, and agentic commerce, positioning it as a leader in payment technology innovation.

Management’s stated priorities focus on accelerating revenue growth through three main pillars: Consumer Payments (CP), Commercial and Money Movement Solutions (CMS), and Value-Added Services (VAS). In CP, the strategy involves strengthening card-based payments while expanding into non-card-based forms, targeting an estimated $20 trillion annual opportunity in underserved spend such as cash, checks, and legacy ACH. Key initiatives include expanding "Tap to Everything" use cases, enhancing token technology, and improving cross-border capabilities. In CMS, the goal is to drive penetration in commercial payments and money movement. In VAS, the focus is on delivering innovative services to deepen client partnerships. Management also emphasizes fortifying the business model’s foundations through investments in infrastructure and strategic acquisitions, such as the purchase of Featurespace Limited for real-time AI payments protection.

The most material risks flagged in the filing are primarily legal and regulatory, specifically the interchange multidistrict litigation (MDL). Visa recorded additional accruals of $2.2 billion and made $875 million in deposits into a U.S. litigation escrow account during fiscal 2025 to address these claims. The estimated interchange reimbursement fees at issue in unresolved claims were approximately $39.4 billion as of October 1, 2025. This litigation is significant enough that it impacts Visa’s capital structure, as the company uses a "U.S. retrospective responsibility plan" to recover monetary liabilities through downward adjustments to the conversion rates of Class B common stock into Class A common stock. Other noted risks include the need to manage higher litigation provisions and personnel expenses, which drove a 30% increase in GAAP operating expenses in fiscal 2025, although management attributes much of this to non-recurring items excluded from non-GAAP measures.
