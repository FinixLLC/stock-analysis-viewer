## Business Profile
Source: 10-K (NVDA_10-K_20260906.txt)

NVIDIA has evolved from a pioneer in accelerated computing and graphics into a data center-scale AI infrastructure company that serves as the foundational backbone for modern artificial intelligence. Its business model is built on a platform strategy that integrates hardware, systems, software, algorithms, and services to address four primary markets: Data Center, Gaming, Professional Visualization, and Automotive. The company reports its results in two segments: Compute Networking, which includes Data Center and Automotive solutions, and Graphics, which covers gaming and professional visualization. In the Data Center segment, NVIDIA provides extreme co-designed infrastructure—combining GPUs, CPUs, DPUs, and high-performance networking—that allows hundreds of thousands of nodes to function as a single giant computer for AI training and inference. This hardware is supported by a deep software stack, most notably the CUDA development platform, which creates a unified programmable architecture that allows NVIDIA to serve diverse industries with shared underlying technology. The company’s recent financial performance reflects this pivot, with fiscal year 2026 revenue reaching $215.9 billion, up 65% year-over-year, driven primarily by a 68% increase in Data Center revenue and the adoption of its Blackwell architecture.

NVIDIA’s competitive moat is derived from its full-stack integration and the resulting ecosystem lock-in. The company emphasizes that its value proposition is not merely its chips, but the holistic optimization of silicon, systems, networking, and software to deliver order-of-magnitude performance advantages over legacy CPU-only approaches. A critical component of this moat is the CUDA ecosystem, which supports over 6,000 applications and has a large, growing developer base that strengthens the platform’s value for customers. This network effect is reinforced by NVIDIA’s position in the high-performance computing space, where it powers over 78% of the supercomputers on the global TOP500 list. Furthermore, the company’s strategy of "extreme co-design," where infrastructure components are architected together to maximize scale and efficiency, creates a barrier to entry that is difficult for competitors to replicate with discrete components. The company also leverages its unified architecture to spread research and development costs across multiple multi-billion-dollar end markets, allowing for leveraged innovation that outpaces traditional Moore’s Law improvements.

Management’s stated priorities center on advancing the accelerated computing platform through rapid innovation and expanding the AI ecosystem. The company maintains a one-year product cadence for Data Center compute introductions, having launched the Blackwell architecture in fiscal year 2025 and the Blackwell Ultra platform in fiscal year 2026, with the Rubin platform expected to commence production shipments in the second half of fiscal year 2027. Beyond hardware, management prioritizes the development of full software stacks and open AI model platforms, such as Nemotron for agentic AI and Cosmos for physical AI, to drive adoption across industries. NVIDIA is also actively investing in the broader AI ecosystem, having invested $17.5 billion in private companies and infrastructure funds in fiscal year 2026, primarily to support early-stage startups and AI model makers that purchase its products. Additionally, the company is focused on supply chain resilience, expanding manufacturing capabilities into the U.S. and Latin America to reduce concentration in Asia, and addressing the complex multi-year challenges of expanding energy capacity to meet the growing demand for AI infrastructure.

The filing flags several material risks that could impact future performance. A primary concern is the availability of data centers, energy, and capital required for customers to build out AI infrastructure; shortages in these resources could delay deployments and reduce the scale of AI adoption. The company also highlights the complexity of product transitions and sophisticated system configurations, which can lead to production delays, supply and demand management challenges, revenue volatility, and quality issues. Regulatory and geopolitical risks are significant, particularly regarding exports to China; NVIDIA incurred a $4.5 billion charge in the first quarter of fiscal year 2026 due to diminished demand for its H20 product following U.S. government export license requirements, and it faces ongoing uncertainty and potential tariffs regarding H200 shipments. Furthermore, the rise of high-quality open-source foundation models poses a competitive threat, as these models could reduce demand for NVIDIA’s products if deployed on competitors’ platforms. Finally, the company notes that its supply chain is heavily concentrated in Asia, and while it is expanding elsewhere, the ability to ramp production in new regions depends on local manufacturing ecosystems
