## Business Profile
Source: 10-K (META_10-K_20260906.txt)

Meta Platforms operates as a technology company with a dual-segment business model, comprising the Family of Apps (FoA) and Reality Labs (RL). The FoA segment, which generates substantially all of the company’s revenue, consists of social and messaging platforms including Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, Messenger, and Threads. The primary revenue driver is the sale of advertising placements to marketers, a model supported by self-service tools and a global sales force. In 2025, FoA generated $198.76 billion in revenue, accounting for 82% of total costs and expenses. The RL segment, which includes VR hardware like Meta Quest, AI glasses, and long-term research into augmented reality and neural interfaces, generated only $2.21 billion in revenue while incurring $21.40 billion in expenses, resulting in an operating loss of $19.19 billion. Management explicitly states that RL is a long-term investment intended to build the "next computing platform," with many products potentially not fully realized for a decade, and expects the segment to remain unprofitable for the foreseeable future, dependent on profits from the FoA segment.

The company’s competitive moat is derived from its massive user base, which reached 3.58 billion daily active people (DAP) in December 2025, and its ability to leverage this scale for advertising effectiveness. Meta competes in connection, sharing, discovery, and AI, facing significant competition from other tech firms in both consumer engagement and frontier AI model development. A key strategic differentiator is the company’s heavy investment in artificial intelligence, including the development of "superintelligence" and open-source models like Llama, which power content ranking, discovery engines, and advertising tools. Additionally, Meta maintains a proprietary technical infrastructure, having designed and built its own data centers and key infrastructure components, although it still relies on third parties for certain components and power. The company also cites its "social by design" product philosophy and organic community growth as foundational to its market position.

Management’s stated priorities for 2026 focus on several key investment areas: AI, Reels and the discovery engine, wearables, monetization of products and services, youth engagement, platform integrity, and infrastructure capacity. The company emphasizes a philosophy of operating efficiently while investing in significant opportunities, with the majority of resources directed toward FoA to support AI initiatives and advertising improvements. Specifically, Meta is investing in generative AI to enhance ad delivery, targeting, and measurement, as well as in privacy-enhancing technologies to navigate regulatory constraints. In the RL segment, the focus is shifting toward wearables, with approximately 70% of 2026 RL operating expenses expected to be allocated to wearables initiatives, including AI glasses and AR prototypes, compared to 30% for VR and Horizon initiatives.

The filing flags several material risks, primarily centered on regulatory and legal challenges that impact advertising revenue. Meta notes that legislative and regulatory developments, including the EU’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), Digital Markets Act, and U.S. state privacy laws, have limited its ability to use data signals for ad targeting and measurement. The company has had to shift its legal basis for behavioral advertising in Europe from "legitimate interests" to "consent," which may reduce ad effectiveness and revenue. Additionally, changes by third-party mobile operating systems and browsers, such as Apple’s iOS updates, have reduced the ability to track user activity, negatively impacting marketer budgets. Other material risks include intense competition, particularly from other products for younger demographics, and the fact that Reels, while growing in usage, monetizes at a lower rate than Feed and Stories. The company also highlights risks related to geopolitical uncertainty, trade volatility, and the substantial capital expenditures required for AI infrastructure, noting that its ability to sustain RL losses depends on the continued profitability of its advertising business.
